First Principles Thinking

Break a problem down to what must be true. In markets, this separates real drivers from inherited opinions, slogans, and copied analysis.

Opportunity Cost

Every choice uses capital, attention, time, and risk budget. The real cost of an action is the best alternative it displaces.

Incentives

People and institutions respond to rewards, penalties, status, career risk, fees, and survival pressure. Incentives often explain behavior before stated beliefs do.

Compounding

Small advantages become large when they persist. Compounding applies to capital, knowledge, reputation, trust, mistakes, and technical debt.

Second-Order Thinking

Ask what happens after the immediate result. Strong decisions account for reactions, feedback, incentives, and delayed effects.

Probabilistic Thinking

Think in odds, ranges, distributions, base rates, and uncertainty. A good decision can lose, and a bad decision can win.

Margin Of Safety

Build room for error because assumptions fail, liquidity changes, people panic, models drift, and unknowns appear at the worst time.

Network Effects

A system becomes more valuable as more participants join. Network effects can create durable advantage, winner-take-most markets, and fragile consensus.

Optionality

Options create upside without forcing full commitment. Optionality is valuable when the future is uncertain and downside is controlled.

Emergence

System-level behavior can appear from many simple interactions. Market trends, cultures, bubbles, and technologies often emerge before anyone fully controls them.

Redundancy

Extra capacity looks inefficient until stress arrives. Redundancy protects systems from single points of failure.

Antifragility

Some systems improve from volatility when losses are limited and learning or adaptation is built in. Fragile systems break, robust systems endure, antifragile systems gain.

Systems thinking platform

How parts become behavior over time.

Systems thinking turns isolated facts into relationships: stocks and flows, constraints, feedback, delays, incentives, adaptation, and nonlinear outcomes.

Complex Systems

Many interacting parts create behavior no single part explains. Markets, economies, companies, and societies are complex systems.

Adaptive Systems

Participants learn, copy, compete, exploit edges, and change the system they are trying to understand.

Feedback Systems

Positive feedback amplifies movement. Negative feedback stabilizes. Confusing the two leads to bad forecasts.

Delayed Effects

Actions can look harmless until consequences arrive later. Monetary policy, leverage, reputation, and habits often work through delay.

Nonlinearity

Small causes can create large effects near thresholds. Liquidity, trust, virality, and panic often behave nonlinearly.

Interdependence

Parts depend on other parts. Supply chains, banking systems, portfolios, and communities can transmit stress through hidden connections.

Leverage Points

Small changes in rules, incentives, defaults, information, or constraints can shift the whole system.

Unintended Consequences

Systems react. Policies, products, and trades can create behaviors nobody intended because incentives changed.
