Separate the scheduled event from the published evidence

A retrospective September 15 research exercise for keeping calendar expectations separate from verified results.

Archive date 15 September 2026. Retrospective note published 17 September 2026.

A calendar is a prompt, not evidence

A scheduled release tells you when to look, not what the result will be. Record the event, its official publisher, and the expected publication time in one field. Leave the result field empty until the source document is available. This prevents a forecast, headline, or social post from being mistaken for a published fact.

Keep three timestamps

For each event, record the scheduled time, the time the original source became available, and the time you retrieved it. These timestamps make later reviews reproducible. They also expose stale commentary that appeared before the underlying release.

Apply the rule to Bitcoin research

A macro calendar can affect the environment around Bitcoin without proving a price direction. Write the mechanism you are testing—liquidity, risk appetite, borrowing costs, or household capacity—and then identify the observation that would support or weaken it. Price alone does not explain the mechanism.

Practice

Choose one event from the official calendar. Write one expected scenario and one contrary scenario, then leave the outcome blank. After publication, copy only the verified result into the evidence field and write your interpretation separately.
